# Exercise-science review for the corpus and graph

Review date and search cutoff: **2026-10-18**.

This focused desk review supports the first generally healthy-adult resistance-
training POC. It is an AI-assisted research synthesis, not an exhaustive systematic
review, individualized exercise prescription, or independent expert approval.
The initial extraction is complete; all proposed claims remain drafts for human
scientific/coaching review. No recommendation policy has been enabled.

The current [requirements](../requirements/00-exercise-graph.md) prioritize the
individual exercise corpus. Programming and circuit findings below remain useful
future context. This review supports the annotation method and some scoped variant
claims; it does not replace exercise-specific anatomy/biomechanics lookup during
curation.

## Main findings

- **Use the 2026 ACSM position stand**, which explicitly updates the 2009 stand.
  Different goals require different claims: maximal strength, muscle size, power,
  and physical function must not be merged into one exercise-quality score.
- **Multiple training approaches work.** Prioritize an achievable programme and
  its stated goals; the highest-ranked research condition is not mandatory for
  every client.
- **Exercise variants matter.** Equipment, joint position, ROM, and execution
  affect what was studied. Shared muscle labels do not establish equivalent
  outcomes or transferable working weights.
- **Track weekly sets, effort, rest, and frequency separately.** Their effects and
  interactions cannot be represented by an exercise name alone.
- **Avoid false precision.** The selected evidence does not validate universal
  muscle-growth percentages, one exact RIR target, an energy-to-kilograms formula,
  or a timeless ranking of the best exercises.

## What was searched and inspected

Europe PMC searches covered current guidance; load; weekly volume/frequency;
failure/proximity; ROM and muscle length; order; rest; equipment; autoregulation;
variation; supersets; EMG interpretation; and two concrete variant comparisons.
Broad searches were followed by more specific title/abstract searches, DOI/PMID
lookups, and targeted correction/retraction checks.

The saved log contains **38 queries**, **373 retrieved record occurrences**, and
**302 unique record IDs**. Each query retrieved only its first page, up to 20
records, using the service's default ordering. These are retrieval counts, not
claims that 302 full texts were screened or that every eligible study was found.
The selected set is purposive and question-led.

Twenty-five substantive sources were selected: **15 accessible full texts** had
relevant methods, results, discussion, and limitations inspected; **10 were
abstract-only**. One additional linked commentary was inspected at metadata level
only. Its arguments have not been appraised. Bibliographic dates, access depth,
source identifiers, passage/table locators, and retrieval hashes are retained.
The [source index](#source-index) links each record below.

The initial scope is adults aged at least 18 without defined disease, doing
general strength/hypertrophy training. Sources containing adolescents, athletes,
or other less-direct samples are explicitly qualified. Rehabilitation,
disease-specific prescriptions, pediatric training, and elite sport optimization
are outside the initial prescribing scope.

This review uses one bibliographic service, bounded searches, and accessible
English-language material. There was no independent second reviewer, exhaustive
pagination, formal AMSTAR/GRADE scoring, or primary-trial reanalysis. Assessments
such as “supported within scope” below are narrative judgments, not formal grades.
The included reviews overlap; their participant totals and effect estimates must
not be added as independent evidence.

Publication types and returned comment/correction links were checked. A targeted
query found no correction/retraction records for several principal titles; this
does not establish an exhaustive publisher audit. The comment on the 2017 volume
review is recorded as a comment, not mislabeled an erratum or retraction.

## Evidence and its implications

### C01–C02: benefits, goals, and loading

The 2026 ACSM stand synthesizes 137 systematic reviews of healthy-adult training.
It supports resistance training for strength, hypertrophy, power, and several
physical-function outcomes. Its search ended in **October 2024**; newer sources
must therefore be considered separately. The 2023 network meta-analysis included
178 studies for strength and 119 for hypertrophy. Both support multiple effective
prescriptions rather than one required programme. [S01, S02]

Higher relative loads generally favor maximal dynamic-strength gains. Hypertrophy
can occur across a wider range of loads, interpreted alongside effort and volume.
The ACSM stand identifies around/above 80% 1RM as favorable for maximal strength;
this is not a requirement that every beginner lift that heavy or that lighter
training cannot help. A network ranking is not a probability of individual success.
Most between-programme estimates in the 2023 review included zero, and its authors
explicitly warn that this does **not** establish equivalence. [S01, S02]

**For the corpus:** store the goal and measurement separately. A strength result
needs the tested exercise/apparatus. Record absolute load, relative load where
available, repetitions, and effort as distinct variables. Do not assign one
scientific “effectiveness” number to every exercise.

### C03–C04: weekly volume and frequency

Higher weekly set volume generally supports greater hypertrophy and strength
across studied ranges, with diminishing returns. The 2026 ACSM stand highlights
higher weekly volume for hypertrophy. Pelland et al., published online in 2025
with a 2026 bibliographic year, analyzed 67 studies and emphasizes that volume and
frequency have different relationships with strength and hypertrophy. This newer
source was available only as an abstract. [S01, S03, S04]

Its fractional accounting of indirect work uses **0.5 as a model convention**;
that is not evidence that every compound set gives every secondary muscle half
of a direct set. Older volume findings must not be extrapolated into a linear
personal muscle-growth forecast. [S03, S04]

For hypertrophy, increasing frequency alone has not shown a clear advantage when
weekly volume is equated. General at-least-twice-weekly participation guidance is
a separate question from the isolated effect of frequency, and strength can have
different responses. Lower-frequency programmes can still be useful. [S01, S03, S22]

**For the graph:** aggregate actual sets by exercise, muscle, and week. Preserve
direct/indirect contribution assumptions as versioned policy. Keep schedule,
weekly volume, and exposure frequency separate; do not hardcode one optimum,
ceiling, or frequency multiplier for every person.

### C05: failure and proximity to failure

Training to momentary muscular failure is **not necessary** for strength or muscle
growth. Failure/non-failure results must still be read with their definitions and
volume/effort differences. The 2022 review's pooled hypertrophy estimate was
SMD 0.22, 95% CI −0.11 to 0.55, and its trained-participant subgroup had only two
studies. That is not proof that the methods are identical. [S01, S05, S07]

An exploratory 2024 meta-regression associated closer estimated proximity to
failure with hypertrophy, but reconstructed RIR from study descriptions and had
modest model fit. A 2026 review found a small dynamic-strength advantage for
non-failure training, SMD 0.24, 95% CI 0.06 to 0.42, with mixed protocols. Neither
result establishes an exact optimal RIR for everyone. [S06, S23]

**For programming:** record set termination and the scale used. Do not force
failure on every set. The ACSM discussion offers near-failure/RIR examples while
also acknowledging insufficient evidence for exact targets; a practical starting
target must not be presented as a universally validated optimum.

### C06–C07: equipment, specificity, and order

The machine/free-weight meta-analysis found strength adaptations specific to the
trained/tested modality and no demonstrated general hypertrophy winner. Trials
were short, hypertrophy measurements sparse, and equipment/protocols varied.
This does not prove every machine is interchangeable with every free-weight
exercise, or that kilograms can be copied between them. [S08]

The exercise-order review found larger strength improvements for exercises placed
earlier in sessions, rather than a universal requirement that multi-joint work
always precede single-joint work. It found no clear general hypertrophy order
effect. Only the abstract was inspected. [S01, S10]

**For selection:** retain apparatus, execution, and test identity. Let a coach's
priority influence exercise order. Model a substitution as a contextual candidate,
with equipment, goal, skill, ROM, and load conventions checked separately.

### C08–C09: rest, supersets, and circuits

The 2024 rest review included nine studies lasting 5–10 weeks. Controlled estimates
slightly favored longer rests but were uncertain: arm SMD 0.13, 95% credible
interval −0.27 to 0.51; thigh 0.17, −0.13 to 0.43. The finding does not make
30–60 seconds mandatory for hypertrophy or establish 90 seconds as an upper
limit. Preserve sufficient recovery for the intended performance. [S09]

The 2025 superset review included 19 studies, but **16 were acute-only**. Only
three studied chronic strength changes and two chronic hypertrophy. Time
efficiency improved in a two-study estimate; perceived effort and pairing type
also mattered. Chronic hypertrophy SMD −0.05, 95% CI −0.63 to 0.54, leaves
substantial uncertainty. Similar-biomechanical pairings can reduce completed
volume relative to traditional sets. [S12]

**For circuits:** distinguish time efficiency, acute work/fatigue, and long-term
outcomes. Store pairing type, transitions, rest, and completed work. Two-exercise
superset evidence is not direct validation of arbitrary three-to-five-exercise
circuits or a claim that antagonist pairings improve long-term results.

### C10–C11: variation, ROM, and muscle length

The exercise-variation review included eight studies and 241 participants, all
young men. It suggests systematic variation can matter, but does not establish a
universal rotation schedule. ACSM also identifies uncertainty here. Expanding the
catalog does not mean clients need constant random novelty. [S01, S11]

ROM and muscle-length evidence needs particular care. An older six-study ROM
review was lower-body-dominant and had limited/conflicting upper-body findings.
A 2025 regional-hypertrophy meta-analysis reported small/trivial differences for
the studied mean-length contrasts, while a later longitudinal-architecture review
suggested potential longer-length advantages but highlighted indirect fascicle
measurements and poor/fair study quality. These reviews do not measure exactly
the same outcome. [S17, S18, S19]

A 2026 narrative review emphasizes the interaction of length and external torque;
it is useful context, not a higher-certainty pooled result or a proof that one
length is always necessary. ACSM's full-ROM strength guidance must not be relabeled
a universal full-ROM hypertrophy or injury-prevention rule. [S01, S21]

**For identity:** retain joint positions, ROM endpoints, support, and resistance
profile where known. Qualify comparative claims by muscle, outcome, and protocol.
Keep exercise continuity when needed to measure progress meaningfully.

### C12–C14: RIR, autoregulation, and EMG

The original RIR-based RPE scale study involved 29 squatters. A later exploratory
accuracy synthesis pooled 12 studies/414 participants and found imperfect
prediction with very high heterogeneity. Its average error is not a correction
constant, and experience did not reliably eliminate error. Both were abstract-only
in this review. [S14, S15]

The selected autoregulation network meta-analysis mixes ages 10–30 and focuses on
squat/bench strength. Its retrieved text reports 438 participants in one section
and 428 in another, and some interval endpoints are malformed. Several reported
comparisons include zero despite broad superiority language. These observations
limit what can be extracted; no numbers have been silently repaired. Its rankings
do not validate a generic pre-session energy-to-load formula. [S16]

The EMG methods review explains why acute excitation measurements alone cannot
rank long-term hypertrophy/strength or supply muscle-growth contribution
percentages. This is a methodological constraint, not a statement that EMG has
no legitimate measurement uses. [S13]

**For client data:** keep pain, readiness/energy, RPE, RIR, and measured performance
separate. Preserve scale definitions and exercise-specific history. Defer automatic
kilogram predictions until a bounded policy has been reviewed and evaluated.

### C15–C18: concrete variants, definitions, and progression

Two primary studies illustrate the required claim granularity:

- **Seated versus prone leg curls:** 20 adults without systematic resistance
  training in the preceding year trained each leg in a different condition for
  12 weeks. Reported whole-hamstring MRI volume increases were about 14% versus
  9%. This supports a scoped comparison, not a universal best-exercise edge or
  clinical hamstring-injury-prevention claim. [S24]
- **Overhead versus neutral-arm cable extensions:** a 21-adult, 12-week
  within-participant comparison reported greater whole-triceps volume growth
  overhead, despite lower lifted loads. Only the abstract was inspected. This
  does not establish transferable working weights or lower injury risk. [S25]

The 2026 definitions review also shows that “resistance exercise” itself has
inconsistent operational meanings. Its proposed definition is conceptual,
not a consensus ontology or validated threshold. Store the actual execution and
stimulus context rather than relying on an exercise name. [S20]

Progression is also contextual. Continued adaptation can require changing the
stimulus, but initial gains do not require a mandatory load increase every
session. A percentage increase used in one trial is a protocol choice, not
independent validation of that increment for every client. [S01, S25]

## Consequences for the POC

The PostgreSQL decision remains appropriate. The science review makes these
data-model distinctions necessary:

| Record | Required distinction |
| --- | --- |
| Exercise revision | Exact apparatus, execution, joint positions, ROM, and loading convention |
| Descriptive annotation | Anatomy/movement label with source, context, and review status |
| Outcome claim | Population, intervention, comparator, outcome, duration, estimate/uncertainty, and source locator |
| Programming policy | Goal, applicability conditions, version, rationale, and approval |
| Session/set observation | Actual load/repetitions, scale-defined exertion, termination, rest, and timestamp |
| Recommendation | Inputs, constraints, corpus/policy versions, explanation, and coach override |

The existing proposal's `SUBSTITUTION_CANDIDATE` semantics should be retained.
Add comparative findings as scoped claims; do not create universal `EQUIVALENT_TO`,
`BEST_FOR`, or scientifically weighted `COMPLEMENTS` edges from tag overlap.

Anatomical muscle-role annotations still need an authoritative anatomy reference
and per-exercise curation. This review has **not** scientifically approved a
50-exercise seed corpus, verified contraindications, or established a canonical
push/pull/hinge taxonomy. Those are distinct tasks from reviewing programming
outcomes. The initial movement categories remain explicit coaching conventions.

The next implementation can build corpus/evidence storage, deterministic imports,
review decisions, filters, and explanations using the draft records. Publishing
exercise annotations and enabling numerical prescribing policies require the
human reviews already specified in `AGENTS.md`; these research files do not
automatically approve themselves. Specific population/exercise gaps should remain
visible rather than being filled with guessed assertions.
